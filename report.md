## 1. Introduction
Finding a parking space in a busy urban area can be frustrating. Drivers may circle blocks, rely on informal advice, encounter full parking lots, or discover restrictions only after arriving. These experiences can increase travel time, fuel use, stress, and the risk of arriving late.

## 2. Problem statement
Urban drivers lack a convenient, trustworthy way to compare nearby parking options using useful information such as location, estimated availability, price, operating hours, and restrictions.

## 3. Objective
The objective of this task is to define representative user needs through personas and an empathy map, identify major pain points, and translate those findings into design opportunities before developing a solution.

## 4. Method
This is an early-stage design exercise using scenario-based assumptions. The personas and empathy map below are illustrative and have not been validated through primary interviews. They should be treated as hypotheses to test with actual drivers.

## 5. User personas

### Persona A: A time-sensitive office commuter
- **Name:** Aarav Sharma (illustrative)
- **Age:** 29
- **Context:** Drives to an office in a busy commercial district.
- **Goals:** Park quickly, arrive on time, and know the likely parking cost.
- **Behaviours:** Checks map apps, asks security guards, and sometimes circles nearby streets.
- **Frustrations:** Unclear availability, unexpected charges, and time lost searching.
- **Needs:** Reliable nearby options, clear pricing, walking distance, and updated availability.

### Persona B: A family visitor
- **Name:** Neha Verma (illustrative)
- **Age:** 36
- **Context:** Drives to markets, clinics, and family activities, sometimes with children or an older relative.
- **Goals:** Find safe, accessible parking close to the destination.
- **Behaviours:** Prefers known lots and asks locals when uncertain.
- **Frustrations:** Long walks, confusing entry points, and uncertainty about accessible spaces.
- **Needs:** Clear directions, safety cues, accessibility information, opening hours, and transparent prices.

## 6. Empathy map

| Dimension | User signals (hypotheses to validate) |
|---|---|
| **Says** | “I need somewhere close”; “How much will it cost?”; “Is there actually space?” |
| **Thinks** | “Will I be late?”; “Is this lot open?”; “Can I trust this information?” |
| **Does** | Circles streets; checks navigation apps; asks guards or shopkeepers; chooses a familiar lot. |
| **Feels** | Rushed, uncertain, frustrated, and relieved when a suitable space is found. |

## 7. Key pain points
1. Availability information may be missing or stale.
2. Parking prices and extra charges may not be clear before arrival.
3. Drivers may not know the best entrance or walking distance.
4. Accessibility and safety information can be hard to compare.
5. Repeated searching adds time, fuel consumption, and stress.

## 8. Design opportunities
- Show nearby parking locations on a map with distance and directions.
- Display prices, opening hours, and restrictions in a consistent format.
- Label availability with a timestamp and confidence level; avoid presenting estimates as guarantees.
- Provide filters for accessible parking, covered parking, and maximum walking distance.
- Offer a clear fallback option if a location is full.

## 9. Success measures for a future prototype
- Time taken to identify a suitable parking option.
- Percentage of users who can understand price and restrictions before choosing.
- User-reported confidence and stress before and after using the prototype.
- Rate of failed arrivals due to inaccurate availability information.

## 10. Conclusion
Personas and empathy mapping help frame the parking problem around people's goals, behaviours, and emotions. The initial design direction is a simple, transparent parking-discovery experience. The next step is to validate the assumptions with real drivers and refine the design based on observed needs.
